# Pitch: <name of the work>

<!-- Five sections, no more. The order matters: a reader should be convinced
     the problem is real before they see the solution, and should know the
     boundaries before they start negotiating scope.
     A pitch is a proposal to be bet on, not a specification. If it reads like
     a ticket breakdown it is too detailed; if it reads like a vision statement
     it is too vague. -->

## 1. Problem

<!-- The raw idea, a use case, or something someone specifically wanted — told
     concretely enough that a reader recognises it.
     One specific story beats a general claim. "Support gets roughly forty
     tickets a month from people who cannot find their invoice" lands; "billing
     UX is poor" does not.
     If you cannot state who has this problem and how you know, stop here and
     go back to the problem/solution canvas. -->

## 2. Appetite

<!-- How much time we are WILLING to spend, chosen before the design exists.
     This is a constraint, not an estimate — the difference is the whole point.
     An estimate answers "how long will this take?" and the design drives the
     number. An appetite answers "how much is this worth?" and the number
     drives the design.
     State it as a fixed budget of time (for example: two weeks with one
     person; or one six-week cycle with a designer and two engineers) and say
     explicitly what happens when the budget runs out: the scope is cut, or the
     bet is abandoned. Never "the deadline moves".
     A human with the authority to spend the budget must set this. An appetite
     derived by an agent from the problem's size is an estimate wearing a
     constraint's clothing. -->
